\chapter{Physical Plant Modeling in Simulink}
\label{ch:simulink_plant}

\section{Introduction to Modeling Approaches}
\label{sec:10_intro}

The preceding chapters treated parameter identification largely as a
signal-processing problem: given a record of current, voltage, and
temperature, recover the coefficients of an equivalent circuit or an
observer gain that best explains the data. This chapter addresses the
complementary problem of \emph{producing} that data in a controlled,
repeatable, and instrumented setting before any embedded estimator is
exposed to a physical cell or to a full vehicle test bench. The
physical plant model built in this chapter is not the identification
algorithm; it is the surrogate battery against which offline and
online identification routines from Chapters~4 through~9 are
exercised, tuned, and stress-tested. The distinction matters
throughout the chapter: the \emph{equivalent circuit model (ECM)}
described in Chapter~2 supplies the mathematical structure of the
plant, while the \emph{Simulink implementation} described here supplies
the numerical environment, current excitation, sensor emulation, and
thermal coupling required to generate realistic terminal measurements.

Three considerations motivate carrying out this step in Simulink
specifically, rather than in a plain MATLAB script or a lower-level
Simscape physical-network description. First, block-diagram
simulation exposes the signal-flow structure of the plant explicitly,
which is convenient both for pedagogical purposes and for later
insertion of an estimator block operating in parallel on the same
signals, as is required for the Software-in-the-Loop (SIL) validation
carried out in Chapter~11. Second, Simulink's fixed-step discrete
solvers allow the plant to be executed at the same sample rate as an
embedded battery management system (BMS), which is a prerequisite for
meaningful real-time feasibility studies (Chapter~7.4 and
Chapter~11.3). Third, the block library approach scales naturally from
a single RC branch (Section~\ref{sec:10_2}) to coupled electro-thermal
representations (Section~\ref{sec:10_3_2}) without requiring a
change of simulation paradigm.

\subsection{The Plant Model as a Distinct Entity from the Identification Model}

A recurring source of confusion in student reports is the conflation
of the plant with the model used to identify it. In this report the
two are kept deliberately separate:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{plant} is the Simulink subsystem intended to
    stand in for the physical cell. It is parameterized once, from a
    reference dataset or literature source, and is thereafter treated
    as ``ground truth'' for the purposes of a given experiment.
    \item The \textbf{identification model} is the structure (for
    example, a recursive least squares filter operating on an assumed
    first-order Thevenin structure) that observes the plant's
    terminal voltage and current and attempts to recover the plant's
    parameters, possibly under a different or deliberately mismatched
    structural assumption.
\end{itemize}

This separation allows a controlled study of structural mismatch,
noise sensitivity, and convergence behavior, none of which can be
examined rigorously if the same equations are used to both generate
and recover the data (an error sometimes referred to informally as
committing an ``inverse crime'' in the parameter estimation
literature). The plant described in this chapter is deliberately built
with enough fidelity, and enough independently sourced parameter data,
that it does not trivially match the assumptions of every identifier
tested against it in later chapters.

\subsection{Chapter Roadmap}

Section~\ref{sec:10_1} develops the top-level simulation architecture:
the current-source excitation, the SOC integrator, the equivalent
circuit subsystem, the sensor noise injection stage, and the logging
interfaces needed for later comparison against estimator outputs.
Section~\ref{sec:10_2} works through the parameterization of a
first-order Thevenin plant in detail, including the discretization of
the RC branch and the two common approaches (lookup table and
polynomial) to embedding SOC-dependent parameters in a block diagram.
Section~\ref{sec:10_3} builds the excitation side of the plant: the
Dynamic Stress Test (DST) framework standardized by the United States
Advanced Battery Consortium (USABC) and the Partnership for a New
Generation of Vehicles (PNGV)/FreedomCAR programs, decomposed into
idle/partial-load segments (Section~\ref{sec:10_3_1}) and high-current
segments coupled to a thermal subsystem for boundary mapping
(Section~\ref{sec:10_3_2}). Section~\ref{sec:10_4} summarizes the
chapter and connects the resulting plant model to the
Software-in-the-Loop validation carried out in Chapter~11.

\section{Simulating the Battery Environment}
\label{sec:10_1}

\subsection{Top-Level Architecture}

The plant model is organized as four coupled subsystems, shown
schematically in the figure plan at the end of this chapter
(Figure~10.1): an excitation subsystem that supplies a current
reference $i(t)$ according to a selected test protocol (constant
current, HPPC pulse train, or DST cycle); an electrical subsystem that
maps $i(t)$ and the present state of charge onto a terminal voltage
$V_t(t)$ through the equivalent circuit of Chapter~2; a
state-of-charge integrator that closes the loop between current and
SOC via coulomb counting; and a thermal subsystem that accepts the
dissipated power from the electrical subsystem and returns a cell
temperature used to modulate the electrical parameters. A fifth,
non-physical block injects sensor noise and quantization consistent
with the acquisition chain discussed in Chapter~3.5, so that any
identification algorithm exercised against the plant experiences
measurement conditions comparable to a real data-acquisition system
rather than idealized noise-free signals.

This layered architecture follows the same separation of concerns
used in early MATLAB/Simulink battery blocks, most notably the
generic dynamic model of Tremblay and Dessaint, whose central design
decision was to use SOC as the \emph{only} integrated state variable
in order to avoid an algebraic loop between the controlled voltage
source and the terminal current \cite{tremblay2007,tremblay2009}. The
same principle is retained here: every quantity that depends
algebraically on SOC (open-circuit voltage, resistances,
capacitances) is computed from the integrator output rather than fed
back through an implicit loop, which keeps the model compatible with
both variable-step and fixed-step discrete solvers.

\subsection{Solver Selection and Timing Considerations}

Two practical choices strongly affect whether a Simulink plant is fit
for identification studies: solver type and step size. A fixed-step
discrete solver operating at the same sample interval $\Delta t$ as
the target embedded estimator (typically 0.1--1~s for automotive BMS
applications) is preferred over a variable-step continuous solver for
three reasons. First, it guarantees that the plant and any
recursive estimator inserted in parallel (Chapters~5--7) advance in
lock-step, which is a precondition for a fair SIL comparison in
Chapter~11. Second, it avoids solver-dependent artifacts in which an
adaptive step size silently refines its resolution around a current
step edge, producing transient behavior that would not be visible to
a real digital controller sampling at a fixed rate. Third, it matches
the assumption made throughout Chapters~5 and~6 that measurements
arrive at discrete instants $t_k = k \Delta t$.

The step size itself must be chosen relative to the fastest time
constant retained in the electrical subsystem. For a first-order
Thevenin branch with polarization resistance $R_1$ and capacitance
$C_1$, the branch time constant is $\tau_1 = R_1 C_1$
(Section~\ref{sec:10_2}), and a working rule of thumb adopted in this
report is
\begin{equation}
\Delta t \le \frac{\tau_{1,\min}}{5}
\label{eq:10_step_rule}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{1,\min}$ is the smallest branch time constant observed
across the SOC and temperature operating range. Equation
\eqref{eq:10_step_rule} is a modeling heuristic rather than a
literature-verified bound and should be treated as a starting point to
be refined by a step-size convergence check (halving $\Delta t$ and
confirming the terminal voltage trajectory changes by less than a
specified tolerance) rather than as a fixed design rule.

\subsection{Sensor Noise and Logging}

Consistent with the acquisition-chain discussion in
Chapter~3.5, the plant model appends a noise-injection stage between
the ``true'' electrical subsystem output and the signal made available
to any identification algorithm. This preserves the important
distinction between the internal state used for thermal boundary
mapping (Section~\ref{sec:10_3_2}), which may be logged noise-free for
diagnostic purposes, and the measured terminal voltage and current
exposed to an estimator, which must include quantization and additive
noise if convergence and robustness studies in Chapters~5--7 are to be
meaningful. All plant states relevant to later chapters (true SOC,
true branch voltages, true cell temperature, dissipated power) are
logged to the base workspace at the solver's sample rate so that
estimator outputs can be compared directly against ground truth
without re-running the plant.

\section{First-Order Thevenin Parameterization}
\label{sec:10_2}

\subsection{Governing Equations}

The plant developed in this section follows the first-order Thevenin
structure already introduced in Chapter~2.2.2 and used repeatedly as
the reference structure for online estimators in Chapters~5 and~6.
Under the sign convention in which discharge current is taken as
positive, the continuous-time state equation for the single RC
(polarization) branch is
\begin{equation}
\frac{dU_1}{dt} = -\frac{U_1}{R_1 C_1} + \frac{i(t)}{C_1}
\label{eq:10_rc_state}
\end{equation}
and the terminal voltage is obtained from Kirchhoff's voltage law as
\begin{equation}
V_t(t) = V_{oc}\big(z(t), T(t)\big) - U_1(t) - i(t) R_0\big(z(t), T(t)\big)
\label{eq:10_terminal}
\end{equation}
where $U_1$ is the voltage across the polarization branch, $R_0$ is
the ohmic (instantaneous) resistance, $R_1$ and $C_1$ are the
polarization resistance and capacitance, $V_{oc}$ is the open-circuit
voltage, $z(t)$ is the state of charge, and $T(t)$ is the cell
temperature. State of charge evolves by coulomb counting,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dz}{dt} = -\frac{\eta \, i(t)}{Q_n}
\label{eq:10_soc}
\end{equation}
with $Q_n$ the nominal capacity and $\eta$ a coulombic efficiency
factor, taken as unity on discharge and slightly below unity on charge
unless a charge-dependent value has been separately identified. This
pair of equations, \eqref{eq:10_rc_state}--\eqref{eq:10_soc},
reproduces the state-space form used for the extended Kalman filter
formulation in Chapter~5.2.1 and is consistent with the Thevenin
model conventions reported for online SOC estimation
\cite{arxiv_ekf_thevenin}.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Implementation}

Because the Simulink plant is executed with a fixed-step discrete
solver (Section~\ref{sec:10_1}), equation \eqref{eq:10_rc_state} is
not integrated with a generic numerical solver but instead
discretized exactly, treating the input current as piecewise constant
over each sample interval (a zero-order-hold assumption). This yields
\begin{equation}
U_{1,k+1} = e^{-\Delta t / \tau_1} \, U_{1,k} + R_1\left(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau_1}\right) i_k, \qquad \tau_1 = R_1 C_1
\label{eq:10_rc_discrete}
\end{equation}
which is implemented in the Simulink subsystem as a single first-order
discrete filter block whose coefficients are recomputed at run time
whenever $R_1$, $C_1$, or $\Delta t$ change with SOC or temperature.
The state-of-charge integrator is discretized identically to
Chapter~5's convention,
\begin{equation}
z_{k+1} = z_k - \frac{\eta \, \Delta t}{Q_n} \, i_k .
\label{eq:10_soc_discrete}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Embedding SOC-Dependent Parameters in the Block Diagram}

$V_{oc}$, $R_0$, $R_1$, and $C_1$ are all, in general, functions of
SOC (and, to a lesser extent, of temperature and current direction).
Two implementation strategies are in common use and are compared in
Table~\ref{tab:10_lookup_vs_poly}.

\textbf{Table-based (lookup) implementation.} Each parameter is stored
as a vector of values at a fixed set of SOC grid points, obtained from
the offline identification procedures of Chapter~4, and Simulink's
1-D lookup table block performs linear (or spline) interpolation
between grid points at run time. This is the approach used, for
example, in the Simulink-based Thevenin parameterization study of
Barletta et al., who tabulated $V_{oc}$, $R_0$, $R_1$, and $C_1$ at
SOC grid points of 20\%, 60\%, and 100\% for three discharge rates on
an NMC~811/graphite commercial cell \cite{barletta2022}. An excerpt of
their reported first-order parameter set is reproduced in
Table~\ref{tab:10_barletta} strictly as an \emph{illustrative literature
example}; it characterizes one specific cell chemistry and format and
must not be reused as a generic parameter set for a different cell
without independent re-identification, a point the source study itself
emphasizes by reporting an estimation error of 7--10\% relative to
directly measured behavior \cite{barletta2022}.

\textbf{Polynomial (curve-fit) implementation.} Each parameter is
instead represented as a low-order polynomial (or a small set of
piecewise polynomials) in SOC, fitted using the ordinary or weighted
least-squares methods of Chapter~4.2. This avoids the need to store
and interpolate a table at run time and produces smoother parameter
trajectories, at the cost of potentially masking sharp, physically
real transitions in $R_0$ or $R_1$ near the extremes of the SOC range.

Two qualitative trends are worth carrying forward into the discussion
of identifiability in later chapters. First, both $R_0$ and $R_1$
decrease monotonically with increasing SOC in the reported dataset,
consistent with reduced ohmic and polarization impedance at higher
lithium content in the negative electrode; the same qualitative trend
is reported independently for a range of ECM structures
\cite{yuan2022_ocv_tau}. Second, $R_0$ is reported to depend
appreciably on discharge C-rate at fixed SOC, which is a reminder that
a plant parameterized from a single current amplitude will not
faithfully reproduce cell behavior across the wide current range
exercised by a DST profile (Section~\ref{sec:10_3}) unless $R_0$ and
$R_1$ are themselves tabulated, or refit, as functions of current
magnitude as well as SOC.

\subsection{Subsystem Decomposition}

For implementation, the first-order Thevenin plant is decomposed into
four Simulink subsystems, corresponding to Figure~10.2 in the figure
plan below:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{OCV subsystem.} Maps the SOC integrator output to
    $V_{oc}(z)$ through a lookup table or polynomial as described
    above.
    \item \textbf{Ohmic-drop subsystem.} Multiplies the instantaneous
    current by $R_0(z, T)$.
    \item \textbf{Polarization-branch subsystem.} Implements the
    discrete recursion of equation \eqref{eq:10_rc_discrete}, with
    $R_1$ and $C_1$ recomputed each step from the current SOC and
    temperature.
    \item \textbf{Summation/output subsystem.} Combines the three
    preceding signals per equation \eqref{eq:10_terminal} and routes
    the result to the noise-injection and logging stage described in
    Section~\ref{sec:10_1}.
\end{enumerate}

This decomposition generalizes directly to the second-order Thevenin
and PNGV structures of Chapter~2.2.3 by adding a second
polarization-branch subsystem in series, and it is consistent with
the block partitioning used in comparable Simulink studies of
Thevenin ECM parameter estimation and identification
\cite{barletta2022,daowd2010,hossain2019}. Where an identification
routine is later required to treat each RC branch as an independent
single-input single-output linear time-invariant system for
system-identification-based extraction, as proposed by Hossain et al.
\cite{hossain2019}, the same subsystem boundary is convenient because
it isolates the branch's current-in, voltage-out behavior from the
OCV and ohmic terms.

\section{Dynamic Stress Testing Framework}
\label{sec:10_3}

\subsection{Purpose and Provenance of the DST Profile}

Where the Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization (HPPC) test of
Chapter~3.3 is designed to characterize a battery's parameters at a
sequence of nominally quasi-static SOC points, the Dynamic Stress Test
(DST) is designed to \emph{exercise} an already-parameterized model,
or a physical cell, under a current profile that resembles the
variable-power demand of a vehicle drive cycle. The DST originates
from the joint USABC/PNGV (later FreedomCAR) battery test procedure
manuals, where it is derived from the Simplified Federal Urban Driving
Schedule (SFUDS) by rescaling to a percentage of a technology's rated
or goal peak power and by increasing the proportion of regenerative
(charging) pulses relative to the SFUDS baseline
\cite{usabc_manual,freedomcar2003}. A single DST repeat unit is
specified as a sequence of discharge and regeneration power (or
current) steps of prescribed relative magnitude and duration, and the
complete test consists of repeating this unit while the plant's SOC is
driven from a fully charged state down through its usable range
\cite{usabc_manual,idaho_dst_report}.

Within this report, the DST framework serves two distinct purposes
that must not be conflated:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identification input.} When applied to a physical
    cell (or to the plant, for algorithm development purposes) with
    the resulting current and voltage records passed to the offline
    or online identification methods of Chapters~4--7, the DST
    provides the persistent excitation needed to identify parameters
    under realistic, continuously varying load, complementing the
    quasi-static HPPC characterization.
    \item \textbf{Validation input.} When a plant has already been
    parameterized from HPPC or OCV data, applying an independently
    recorded DST or drive-cycle profile (for example, the Federal
    Urban Driving Schedule, FUDS, used alongside DST in several
    published SOC-estimation studies) and comparing simulated versus
    measured terminal voltage provides an out-of-sample validation of
    the plant's fidelity \cite{exogenous_kf_soc,tsmo_soh}.
\end{itemize}

Both roles reappear directly in Chapter~11, where the same DST
excitation is applied to the compiled plant plus BMS estimator in a
Software-in-the-Loop configuration to evaluate transient tracking
performance.

\subsection{Implementing the DST Profile in Simulink}

Two implementation patterns are available for driving the plant's
current-excitation subsystem with a DST profile:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{time-indexed lookup table}, in which the
    normalized power (or current) values specified by the USABC/PNGV
    DST schedule are stored against elapsed time within one repeat
    unit, and a repeating-sequence or modulo-time block cycles through
    the table for as many repeat units as are required to traverse the
    desired SOC range. This is the simpler implementation and is
    adequate whenever the profile is to be applied open-loop, without
    early termination logic.
    \item A \textbf{Stateflow-based sequencer}, in which each segment
    of the DST repeat unit (acceleration, cruise, regeneration, idle)
    is represented as a state, and transitions between states are
    triggered by elapsed time \emph{or} by plant feedback such as a
    minimum terminal voltage or a target SOC. This pattern is required
    whenever the test must terminate adaptively, for example when the
    plant's terminal voltage first reaches a specified cutoff, which
    is how the physical USABC procedure itself defines end-of-test
    \cite{usabc_manual}.
\end{enumerate}

Because power, not current, is the quantity specified in the USABC DST
schedule, the excitation subsystem must convert the scheduled power
fraction into a current command using the plant's present terminal
voltage, $i_{cmd}(t) = P_{cmd}(t) / V_t(t)$, which introduces a mild
algebraic coupling between the excitation and electrical subsystems.
In this report this coupling is broken by using the terminal voltage
from the \emph{previous} sample instant in the conversion, consistent
with the fixed-step, zero-order-hold discretization already adopted
for the electrical subsystem in Section~\ref{sec:10_2}; the resulting
one-step delay is negligible relative to the branch time constants
identified in Chapter~4 provided the sample interval satisfies
equation \eqref{eq:10_step_rule}.

\subsection{Idle and Partial Load Scenarios}
\label{sec:10_3_1}

The lower-stress segments of a DST repeat unit, and the rest periods
that separate HPPC pulses, serve a purpose in plant development that
is easy to overlook when attention is focused on peak-power
performance: they are the primary source of information about the
polarization time constant $\tau_1 = R_1 C_1$. When the excitation
current is driven to zero, equation \eqref{eq:10_rc_discrete}
predicts that the branch voltage $U_1$ relaxes exponentially toward
zero with time constant $\tau_1$, so a logged rest-period voltage
trace can be fit directly (using the curve-fitting or ordinary
least-squares methods of Chapter~4) to recover $R_1$ and $C_1$
independently of the ohmic term $R_0$, which only affects the
instantaneous step at the moment current is interrupted. This
relaxation-based extraction is the classical counterpart to the
system-identification-based extraction of Hossain et al., who instead
fit the RC branch as a discrete-time linear system directly from
pulse-response data \cite{hossain2019}.

Within the Simulink plant, idle and partial-load segments are
represented as short sub-blocks of the excitation subsystem:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Idle (rest) segments}: current commanded to zero for
    a fixed duration (typically tens of seconds to tens of minutes,
    depending on whether the segment is a brief DST idle step or a
    full inter-pulse rest of the kind specified for HPPC), used both
    to allow the plant's OCV to relax toward its equilibrium value and
    to expose the $U_1$ relaxation curve for offline fitting.
    \item \textbf{Partial-load segments}: current commanded to a
    moderate fraction (illustratively, 10--40\%) of the profile's peak
    magnitude, used to probe the mid-range linearity of the
    polarization branch without invoking the higher-order nonlinear
    effects that dominate at high C-rate (Section~\ref{sec:10_3_2}).
\end{itemize}

Because the idle-segment fit and the HPPC-based fit of Chapter~4 use
the same underlying relaxation physics but different excitation
histories, comparing the two provides a simple, low-cost
identifiability check: if $R_1$ and $C_1$ recovered from DST idle
segments differ substantially from those recovered from dedicated
HPPC rest periods, this is evidence either of an SOC- or
history-dependence not captured by the current model order, or of
insufficient excitation richness in one of the two records, a concern
directly connected to the identifiability discussion of
Chapter~4.3/Chapter~12.

\subsection{High-Current Stress and Thermal Boundary Mapping}
\label{sec:10_3_2}

\subsubsection{Coupling the Electrical and Thermal Subsystems}

The high-magnitude discharge and regenerative pulses present in a
scaled DST profile are the segments most likely to reveal nonlinear
resistance growth and to produce non-negligible self-heating, and they
are therefore the natural point at which to couple the electrical
subsystem of Section~\ref{sec:10_2} to the lumped thermal model
introduced structurally in Chapter~2.4. The heat generation rate
supplied to the thermal subsystem is computed using the energy-balance
form attributed to Bernardi, Pawlikowski, and Newman, which decomposes
total heat generation into an irreversible (Joule) term and a
reversible (entropic) term,
\begin{equation}
\dot Q(t) = i(t)^2 \Big[R_0(z,T) + R_1(z,T)\Big] \;-\; i(t)\, T(t) \, \frac{\partial V_{oc}}{\partial T}
\label{eq:10_bernardi}
\end{equation}
where $\partial V_{oc}/\partial T$ is the entropic (open-circuit
voltage temperature) coefficient \cite{bernardi1985}. Equation
\eqref{eq:10_bernardi} is implemented as an additional subsystem that
reads the present current and branch resistances from the electrical
subsystem and the present cell temperature from the thermal
subsystem's own state, closing a second, thermal feedback loop around
the plant in addition to the SOC loop of Section~\ref{sec:10_1}.

Two caveats from the thermal-modeling literature are carried forward
explicitly rather than absorbed silently into the plant. First, the
relative contribution of the entropic term is small at low C-rate but
becomes material at high C-rate, with one study reporting a cell
surface temperature difference of up to approximately 7~K at a 3C
discharge rate attributable to including versus excluding the entropic
term at 25\textdegree C ambient \cite{chun2024_entropic}, which
supports retaining the second term of equation \eqref{eq:10_bernardi}
specifically for the high-current segments addressed in this
subsection, even where it might reasonably be neglected in the
low-current idle/partial-load segments of Section~\ref{sec:10_3_1}.
Second, the Bernardi formulation itself is known to lose accuracy
under realistic pulse-discharge excitation relative to continuous
discharge, with reported deviations from direct calorimetric
measurement reaching tens of percent for some chemistries under
high-rate pulsing \cite{bernardi_pulse_accuracy}; this is noted here
as a limitation of the plant's thermal fidelity rather than corrected
for, since a pulse-corrected heat-generation model is outside the
first-order scope adopted for this report and would itself require
independent identification of additional coefficients.

\subsubsection{Boundary-Mapping Procedure}

``Thermal boundary mapping,'' as used in this report, refers to a
structured sweep of the coupled electrical-thermal plant intended to
characterize, in simulation, the relationship between applied current
magnitude, pulse duration, ambient temperature, and resulting cell
temperature rise. The procedure is summarized in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:10_boundary_map}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Simulated thermal boundary mapping using the coupled plant}
\label{alg:10_boundary_map}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Inputs:} candidate current magnitudes $\{i_1,\dots,i_m\}$; candidate pulse durations $\{d_1,\dots,d_n\}$; ambient temperatures $\{T_{a,1},\dots,T_{a,p}\}$; maximum allowable temperature rise $\Delta T_{lim}$ (a design or safety input supplied externally, not derived by the plant itself)
\State \textbf{Outputs:} map of simulated temperature rise $\Delta T(i, d, T_a)$; boundary current $i_{lim}(d, T_a)$ satisfying $\Delta T \le \Delta T_{lim}$
\For{each ambient temperature $T_{a,k}$}
    \State Initialize plant at a fixed reference SOC and set $T(0) = T_{a,k}$
    \For{each pulse duration $d_j$}
        \For{each current magnitude $i_\ell$}
            \State Apply a discharge pulse of magnitude $i_\ell$ for duration $d_j$ using the excitation subsystem of Section~\ref{sec:10_3}
            \State Log $\Delta T = T(d_j) - T_{a,k}$ from the thermal subsystem state
            \State Record $\dot Q(t)$ from equation \eqref{eq:10_bernardi} for diagnostic purposes
        \EndFor
        \State Interpolate across $\{i_\ell\}$ to estimate $i_{lim}(d_j, T_{a,k})$ such that $\Delta T = \Delta T_{lim}$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return the tabulated map $\Delta T(i,d,T_a)$ and boundary curve $i_{lim}(d,T_a)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The output of Algorithm~\ref{alg:10_boundary_map} is a set of
simulated safe-operating boundary curves that are used in two ways
later in this report. Within this chapter, they provide a basis for
selecting the high-current segment magnitudes used in the DST
excitation subsystem so that the plant is exercised close to, but not
arbitrarily beyond, the electrical-thermal regime for which its
parameters remain reasonably valid. In Chapter~11, the same boundary
data motivates the transient-tracking scenarios used to evaluate
estimator behavior around abrupt load disconnects and high-current
excursions. It is emphasized that any specific numerical boundary
($\Delta T_{lim}$, or the resulting $i_{lim}$ values) generated by this
procedure is a property of the particular plant parameterization and
thermal subsystem coefficients chosen for a given study; unless those
coefficients have themselves been independently validated against
calorimetric or manufacturer data for the cell under study, the
resulting boundary map should be treated as illustrative of the
\emph{method} rather than as a validated safety limit for any real
cell.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:10_4}

This chapter converted the equivalent circuit structures and offline
characterization procedures of Chapters~2--4 into an executable
Simulink plant suitable for exercising the identification algorithms
developed in Chapters~4 through~9. The plant was organized around a
clean separation between excitation, electrical, state-of-charge, and
thermal subsystems, with SOC retained as the only integrated state in
the electrical path to avoid algebraic-loop issues, following the
design principle established for early generic MATLAB/Simulink
battery models \cite{tremblay2007,tremblay2009}. The first-order
Thevenin parameterization was developed in discretized form suitable
for a fixed-step solver, and two practical strategies for embedding
SOC-dependent parameters — lookup tables and polynomial fits — were
compared, with an illustrative literature-sourced parameter set
included strictly as an example of typical magnitudes and trends
rather than as a reusable dataset \cite{barletta2022}. The Dynamic
Stress Test framework standardized by USABC and PNGV/FreedomCAR was
then implemented as the plant's primary dynamic excitation, and its
idle/partial-load segments were shown to support relaxation-based
extraction of the polarization time constant, while its high-current
segments were coupled to a Bernardi-based thermal subsystem to support
a structured thermal boundary-mapping procedure, with explicit caveats
regarding the accuracy limits of that heat-generation model under
pulsed excitation.

The plant built in this chapter is deliberately kept independent of
any single identification algorithm so that it can serve as a common,
reusable test environment. Chapter~11 takes up this plant directly,
coupling it in a Software-in-the-Loop configuration to a representative
BMS estimator to evaluate transient tracking performance around abrupt
load disconnects, dynamic voltage-peak stabilization, and real-time
computational efficiency under the same DST and thermal-boundary
excitation developed here.
