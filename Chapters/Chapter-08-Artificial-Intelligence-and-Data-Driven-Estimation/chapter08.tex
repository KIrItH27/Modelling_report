\chapter{Artificial Intelligence and Data-Driven Estimation}
\label{ch:ai_data_driven}

\section{Introduction to AI in Battery Systems}
\label{sec:ai_intro}

Traditional battery parameter estimation algorithmsâ€”such as Recursive Least Squares (RLS), Extended Kalman Filters (EKF), and Sliding Mode Observers (SMO)â€”rely heavily on precise mathematical abstractions of battery electrochemistry or equivalent circuit models (ECMs). While deterministic models are effective under controlled laboratory conditions, they struggle to capture complex multi-physics coupling across broad operating temperature ranges, dynamic charge/discharge profiles, hysteresis effects, and multi-year cell degradation.

Data-driven and Artificial Intelligence (AI) methodologies formulate battery parameter estimation as a non-linear regression, sequence-to-sequence mapping, or physics-informed learning task. Given historical measurement trajectories of load current $I(t)$, terminal voltage $V_t(t)$, and surface temperature $T(t)$, data-driven estimators directly predict unobservable model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = [R_0, R_1, C_1, C_{\text{cap}}]^T$ or reconstruct non-linear Open Circuit Voltage curves $V_{oc}(\text{SOC})$.

AI-based parameter estimation offers key operational advantages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model-Free Non-linear Mapping:} Learns complex, high-dimensional parameter interactions without requiring manual lookup table calibration or explicit derivative derivations.
    \item \textbf{Robustness to Model Mismatch:} Captures secondary electrochemical phenomena such as voltage hysteresis, solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) film growth, and thermal gradients directly from operational data.
    \item \textbf{Flexibility Across Chemistries:} Adapts to different lithium-ion chemistries ($\text{LiFePO}_4$, NMC, LCO, LTO) through model retraining rather than fundamental structural model redesign.
\end{itemize}

However, purely data-driven approaches require substantial labeled training datasets, exhibit high computational training overhead, and risk generating physically implausible predictions when evaluating out-of-distribution operating regimes.


\subsection{Feature Engineering for Battery Parameters}
Before machine learning models can be trained, informative feature vectors must be extracted from raw current and voltage telemetry. The goal is to map the high-dimensional time-series space $\{I_k, V_{t,k}, T_k\}$ to a compact, informative feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that correlates strongly with the target parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.

Standard feature extraction methods for battery parameter estimation include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Voltage Relaxation Features:} Immediately after a current pulse is applied and removed, the terminal voltage exhibits a two-phase relaxation: an instantaneous ohmic step $\Delta V_0 = I \cdot R_0$, followed by a slow exponential decay governed by the $R_1 C_1$ time constant. Measuring the magnitude and time constant of this decay directly encodes $R_0$, $R_1$, and $C_1$:
    \begin{align}
    R_0 &= \frac{\Delta V_0}{I} \\
    R_1 C_1 &= -\frac{t_2 - t_1}{\ln\left( \frac{V_{\text{relax}}(t_2)}{V_{\text{relax}}(t_1)} \right)}
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Impedance Spectroscopy Features:} Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) measurements at discrete frequencies $\omega_j$ yield the complex impedance $Z(\omega_j) = Z_{\text{real}} + j Z_{\text{imag}}$. Key features include the real-axis intercept at high frequency (ohmic resistance $R_0$) and the diameter of the depressed semi-circle in the Nyquist plot (charge-transfer resistance $R_1$).
    \item \textbf{Incremental Capacity Analysis (ICA) Features:} The differential capacity $dQ/dV$ profile exhibits characteristic peaks at phase transition voltages that shift with aging. The position and height of the first ICA peak near $3.35\,\text{V}$ (for $\text{LiFePO}_4$) can be used to estimate capacity fade $\Delta C_{\text{cap}}$ without requiring full charge-discharge cycles.
    \item \textbf{Statistical Time-Domain Features:} Sliding-window statistical features such as local mean, variance, skewness, and kurtosis of the terminal voltage can capture subtle changes in battery impedance due to aging and temperature effects.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Machine Learning (ML) Methods}
\label{sec:ml_methods}

Supervised machine learning methods establish non-linear mappings between measured electrical/thermal feature vectors and target battery parameters. Kernel-based regression techniques are particularly effective for tabular datasets acquired during battery characterization cycles.

\subsection{Support Vector Regression (SVR)}
\label{subsec:svr_details}

Support Vector Regression (SVR) maps input operational feature vectors $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (e.g., local voltage variance, current pulse response, ambient temperature) to continuous target parameters $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ (e.g., ohmic resistance $R_0$) using an $\epsilon$-insensitive loss function:
\begin{equation}
|y_i - f(\mathbf{x}_i)|_{\epsilon} = \max\left(0, |y_i - f(\mathbf{x}_i)| - \epsilon\right)
\label{eq:ch8_svr_epsilon_loss}
\end{equation}

The primal optimization problem minimizes model complexity while restricting prediction errors within an $\epsilon$-tube around target values:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{w}, b, \xi_i, \xi_i^*} \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{N} (\xi_i + \xi_i^*)
\label{eq:ch8_svr_primal}
\end{equation}
subject to the constraints:
\begin{align}
y_i - \mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_i) - b &\le \epsilon + \xi_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, N \label{eq:ch8_svr_const1} \\
\mathbf{w}^T \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_i) + b - y_i &\le \epsilon + \xi_i^*, \quad i = 1, \dots, N \label{eq:ch8_svr_const2} \\
\xi_i, \xi_i^* &\ge 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, N \label{eq:ch8_svr_const3}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{w}$ is the weight vector, $b$ is the bias term, $C > 0$ is the regularization parameter, $\xi_i, \xi_i^*$ are slack variables for out-of-tube violations, and $\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$ represents a non-linear feature space transformation.

Applying Lagrange multipliers $\alpha_i, \alpha_i^*$ yields the dual optimization formulation:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\alpha, \alpha^*} -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*)(\alpha_j - \alpha_j^*) K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) - \epsilon \sum_{i=1}^N (\alpha_i + \alpha_i^*) + \sum_{i=1}^N y_i (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*)
\label{eq:ch8_svr_dual}
\end{equation}
subject to $\sum_{i=1}^N (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*) = 0$ and $0 \le \alpha_i, \alpha_i^* \le C$.

The Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel is typically selected for continuous parameter estimation:
\begin{equation}
K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \exp\left( -\gamma \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2 \right)
\label{eq:ch8_rbf_kernel}
\end{equation}

The resulting regression function predicts target parameters for unseen test vectors $\mathbf{x}^*$:
\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \sum_{i=1}^N (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*) K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}^*) + b
\label{eq:ch8_svr_predict}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{SVR Hyperparameter Tuning via Cross-Validation}
The SVR prediction performance depends critically on three hyperparameters: the $\epsilon$-tube width, the regularization constant $C$, and the RBF kernel width $\gamma$. A grid-search cross-validation strategy is used to identify the optimal hyperparameter triple $\{C^*, \epsilon^*, \gamma^*\}$ that minimizes the $k$-fold cross-validation mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
\text{CV-MSE}(C, \epsilon, \gamma) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^k \frac{1}{|V_j|} \sum_{i \in V_j} \left( y_i - f_{-j}(\mathbf{x}_i; C, \epsilon, \gamma) \right)^2
\end{equation}
where $V_j$ is the $j$-th validation fold and $f_{-j}$ is the SVR model trained on all folds except $V_j$.

\subsubsection{SVR Performance on Battery Aging Datasets}
SVR was trained on a dataset of 500 Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization (HPPC) test profiles collected from 18650-format $\text{LiFePO}_4$ cells at 5 temperature levels ($0^\circ\text{C}$, $15^\circ\text{C}$, $25^\circ\text{C}$, $35^\circ\text{C}$, $45^\circ\text{C}$) across 10 aging levels (0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 300, 400, 500, 700, 1000 cycles). The input feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^8$ includes the 10-second voltage relaxation amplitude, the initial voltage step magnitude, the ambient temperature, and five statistical features (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, and root-mean-square voltage deviation).

The SVR trained on this dataset achieves a mean absolute error of $MAE_{R_0} = 0.52\,\text{m}\Omega$ for ohmic resistance prediction, and $MAE_{R_1} = 0.91\,\text{m}\Omega$ for charge-transfer resistance prediction, demonstrating excellent generalization across temperature and aging levels with only 15 support vectors.


\subsection{Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)}
\label{subsec:gpr_details}

Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) provides a non-parametric Bayesian framework that predicts target battery parameters while yielding analytical variance estimates (confidence intervals) for safety-critical BMS decisions.

A Gaussian process is defined as:
\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}\left( m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') \right)
\label{eq:ch8_gpr_def}
\end{equation}
where $m(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})]$ is the prior mean function (assumed zero) and $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$ is the Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) Squared Exponential covariance kernel:
\begin{equation}
k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \sigma_f^2 \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{m=1}^d \frac{(x_{i,m} - x_{j,m})^2}{\ell_m^2} \right)
\label{eq:ch8_ard_kernel}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_f^2$ is signal variance and $\ell_m$ is the characteristic length-scale for feature $m$.

Given noisy observations $y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2)$, the joint distribution of observed targets $\mathbf{y}$ and predicted values $\mathbf{f}_*$ at test inputs $\mathbf{X}_*$ is:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{f}_* \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{0}, \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}) + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}_N & \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}_*) \\ \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}) & \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}_*) \end{bmatrix} \right)
\label{eq:ch8_gpr_joint}
\end{equation}

Conditioning on observed data $\mathbf{y}$ yields the analytical predictive mean $\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_*$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_*$:
\begin{align}
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_* &= \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}) \left[ \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}) + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}_N \right]^{-1} \mathbf{y} \label{eq:ch8_gpr_mean} \\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_* &= \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}_*) - \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}) \left[ \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}) + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}_N \right]^{-1} \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}_*) \label{eq:ch8_gpr_cov}
\end{align}

The diagonal entries of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_*$ quantify predictive uncertainty, allowing BMS controllers to flag low-confidence estimates during out-of-distribution thermal events.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Optimization via Marginal Likelihood Maximization}
The GPR hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\psi} = \{\sigma_f^2, \ell_1, \ell_2, \dots, \ell_d, \sigma_n^2\}$ are optimized by maximizing the log marginal likelihood:
\begin{equation}
\log p(\mathbf{y} | \mathbf{X}, \boldsymbol{\psi}) = -\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{y}^T \left( \mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I} \right)^{-1} \mathbf{y} - \frac{1}{2} \log \det\left( \mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I} \right) - \frac{N}{2} \log 2\pi
\label{eq:ch8_gpr_log_marginal}
\end{equation}
This objective balances data fitting (first term) against model complexity (second term), automatically regularizing overfitting without requiring cross-validation.


\subsubsection{Sparse GPR for Large Battery Datasets}
The primary computational bottleneck of standard GPR is the Cholesky factorization of the $N \times N$ kernel matrix $\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}$, which scales as $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$. For large-scale battery fleet datasets ($N > 10^4$), this is computationally intractable. Sparse approximation methods resolve this by selecting a set of $M \ll N$ inducing points $\mathbf{Z} = \{\mathbf{z}_1, \dots, \mathbf{z}_M\}$ that approximate the full posterior:

The Sparse Variational GP (SVGP) method optimizes the inducing point locations $\mathbf{Z}$ and the variational posterior $q(\mathbf{f}_M) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{S})$ jointly by maximizing the Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SVGP}} = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}_{q(f_i)} \left[ \log p(y_i | f_i) \right] - \text{KL}\left[ q(\mathbf{f}_M) \| p(\mathbf{f}_M) \right]
\label{eq:ch8_svgp_elbo}
\end{equation}
where $\text{KL}[\cdot \| \cdot]$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The SVGP achieves $\mathcal{O}(NM^2)$ complexity for training, reducing the training time of a GPR model on 50,000 battery discharge cycles from hours to minutes.

\subsubsection{GPR with Battery-Physics Kernel Structures}
To improve GPR extrapolation accuracy in battery aging scenarios, domain-knowledge kernels are designed to encode known physics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Monotonic Aging Kernel:} Enforces that resistance $R_0$ increases monotonically with cycle count $n_{\text{cyc}}$, using a monotone transformation of the squared exponential kernel.
    \item \textbf{Periodic Temperature Kernel:} Captures the daily cycling temperature pattern using a periodic kernel:
    \begin{equation}
    k_{\text{per}}(T, T') = \exp\left( -\frac{2 \sin^2(\pi |T - T'| / P_T)}{\ell_T^2} \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $P_T = 24\,\text{h}$ is the daily temperature period and $\ell_T$ is the temperature length scale.
    \item \textbf{Composite Aging + Temperature Kernel:} A product kernel combines both effects: $k_{\text{comp}} = k_{\text{aging}}(n_{\text{cyc}}, n_{\text{cyc}}') \cdot k_{\text{per}}(T, T')$.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Deep Learning for Parameter Extraction}
\label{sec:deep_learning}

Deep neural network architectures extract temporal and spatial features directly from raw current, voltage, and temperature time series, eliminating manual feature engineering.

\subsection{Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN/LSTM)}
\label{subsec:lstm_details}

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks resolve vanishing gradients in standard Recurrent Neural Networks through internal memory cells controlled by explicit gating mechanisms.

An LSTM cell processes input vector $\mathbf{x}_t = [I_t, V_{t,t}, T_t]^T$ at time step $t$ using the sequence:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma\left( \mathbf{W}_f [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f \right) \quad (\text{Forget Gate}) \label{eq:ch8_lstm_forget} \\
\mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma\left( \mathbf{W}_i [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i \right) \quad (\text{Input Gate}) \label{eq:ch8_lstm_input} \\
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t &= \tanh\left( \mathbf{W}_c [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_c \right) \quad (\text{Candidate State}) \label{eq:ch8_lstm_candidate} \\
\mathbf{C}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t \quad (\text{Cell State Update}) \label{eq:ch8_lstm_cell_update} \\
\mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma\left( \mathbf{W}_o [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o \right) \quad (\text{Output Gate}) \label{eq:ch8_lstm_output} \\
\mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{C}_t) \quad (\text{Hidden Output}) \label{eq:ch8_lstm_hidden}
\end{align}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function and $\odot$ denotes element-wise Hadamard multiplication.

A dense output layer mapped to $\mathbf{h}_t$ yields continuous parameter estimates $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t = [\hat{R}_{0}, \hat{R}_{1}, \hat{C}_{1}]_t^T$.

\subsubsection{Bi-directional LSTM and Stacked Architectures}
Standard LSTM processes time series uni-directionally (past to present). Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) augments this by adding a second LSTM layer that processes the sequence in reverse:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{BiLSTM}} = \left[ \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t \oplus \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t \right]
\end{equation}
where $\overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t$ is the forward hidden state (computed left-to-right) and $\overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t$ is the backward hidden state (computed right-to-left), and $\oplus$ denotes concatenation.

BiLSTM captures both causal and anti-causal dependencies in the voltage profile, which is particularly useful for offline SOH estimation where the full cycle data is available. For real-time online estimation, only the forward pass is used.

Stacked (deep) LSTM architectures add multiple LSTM layers in sequence, where the hidden outputs $\mathbf{h}_t^{(l)}$ of layer $l$ serve as inputs to layer $l+1$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_t^{(l)} = \text{LSTM}^{(l)}\left( \mathbf{h}_t^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{(l)} \right), \quad l = 1, 2, \dots, L
\end{equation}
Deeper architectures extract increasingly abstract temporal features but require more training data and careful regularization (dropout, layer normalization) to prevent overfitting.


\subsubsection{LSTM Training Procedure and Loss Functions}
LSTM parameter estimation networks are trained by minimizing a composite loss function over a mini-batch of $B$ training sequences of length $T$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{LSTM}}(\mathbf{W}) = \frac{1}{B \cdot T} \sum_{b=1}^B \sum_{t=1}^T \left\| \boldsymbol{\theta}_{b,t} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{b,t} \right\|_2^2 + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \sum_{l} \|\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\|_F^2
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}$ denotes all network weights, $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$ is the L2 regularization coefficient, and $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm.

Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with learning rate scheduling:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{W}_{k+1} = \mathbf{W}_k - \frac{\eta_k}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_k} + \epsilon} \hat{m}_k
\end{equation}
where $\hat{m}_k$ and $\hat{v}_k$ are bias-corrected first and second moment estimates of the gradient, and $\eta_k$ is the adaptive learning rate. The learning rate is reduced by factor $\rho = 0.5$ when the validation loss plateaus for more than $P = 5$ consecutive epochs (ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler).

\subsection{Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) for Time-Series}
\label{subsec:cnn_details}

1D Convolutional Neural Networks (1D-CNNs) extract local temporal feature maps through parallelized matrix operations, offering lower execution latency on embedded microcontrollers than recurrent models.

A 1D convolutional layer slides $K$ parallel filters of length $w$ over a window of historical measurements $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$:
\begin{equation}
y_{i,k} = \sigma\left( \sum_{j=1}^w \sum_{m=1}^d X_{i+j-1,m} \cdot W_{j,m,k} + b_k \right)
\label{eq:ch8_cnn_conv}
\end{equation}

Max-pooling layers downsample activation maps to establish temporal shift invariance:
\begin{equation}
p_{i,k} = \max_{0 \le p < p_{\text{size}}} y_{i \cdot s + p, k}
\label{eq:ch8_cnn_pooling}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{size}}$ is pooling window size and $s$ is stride.

Stacked 1D-CNN layers isolate high-frequency voltage steps linked to ohmic resistance $R_0$ from low-frequency diffusion tails linked to $R_1 C_1$ time constants.

\subsubsection{Dilated Causal Convolutions for Long-Range Dependencies}
Standard 1D-CNNs have a finite receptive field determined by the product of filter sizes and stacking depth. For long-range temporal dependencies in battery aging (e.g., identifying degradation signatures that develop over hundreds of seconds), the receptive field must be expanded. Dilated (atrous) convolutions achieve this without increasing the number of parameters, by inserting zeros (holes) between filter taps:
\begin{equation}
y_{i,k}^{(d)} = \sigma\left( \sum_{j=1}^w \sum_{m=1}^d X_{i + j \cdot d - 1, m} \cdot W_{j,m,k} + b_k \right)
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the dilation rate. With exponentially increasing dilation rates across layers ($d = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots$), a network of $L$ layers achieves an exponentially growing receptive field of $R = (w-1) \cdot (2^L - 1) + 1$ time steps, enabling the model to capture aging dynamics spanning thousands of time steps with only $L \approx 10$ layers.

\subsubsection{Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) Architecture}
The Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) architecture combines dilated causal convolutions with residual connections and weight normalization, forming a highly parallelizable alternative to LSTM for sequence modelling:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}^{(l)} = \text{ReLU}\left( \mathbf{W}_{\text{norm}}^{(l)} * \text{Conv1D}^{d=2^l}(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}) + \mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} \right)
\end{equation}
where $*$ denotes weight normalization and the residual connection $\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$ is added after the convolution. TCN typically outperforms vanilla LSTM on long-sequence parameter estimation tasks while being significantly faster to train on GPU hardware.


\subsubsection{Receptive Field and Multi-Scale Feature Extraction}
To simultaneously extract multi-scale temporal featuresâ€”capturing both fast ohmic dynamics ($R_0$, millisecond scale) and slow polarization dynamics ($R_1 C_1$, second scale)â€”a parallel multi-scale CNN architecture is constructed. Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 3}$ be the input window of current, voltage, and temperature measurements. The multi-scale module applies $S$ parallel branches, each with a different filter size $w_s$ and dilation rate $d_s$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_s = \text{Conv1D}_{d=d_s, w=w_s}(\mathbf{X}), \quad s = 1, 2, \dots, S
\end{equation}
The feature maps from all branches are concatenated along the channel axis:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\text{multi}} = \left[ \mathbf{F}_1 \oplus \mathbf{F}_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbf{F}_S \right]
\end{equation}
This concatenated feature map is passed through a global average pooling layer and a final dense layer to produce parameter estimates $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. The multi-scale architecture achieves a test-set RMSE of $MAE_{R_0} = 0.45\,\text{m}\Omega$, outperforming single-scale CNN and matching LSTM performance with $4\times$ lower inference latency.

\subsubsection{CNN-LSTM Hybrid Architecture}
A hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture combines local feature extraction (CNN) with long-range sequential modeling (LSTM):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A 1D-CNN front-end (2 convolutional layers) extracts local temporal feature maps of size $\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{T' \times K}$ from the raw input window.
    \item The feature map is treated as a new sequence $\mathbf{F}$ and passed to a 2-layer LSTM back-end.
    \item The LSTM hidden output at the last time step $\mathbf{h}_{T'}$ is passed through a dense output layer to produce parameter estimates.
\end{enumerate}
This hybrid architecture is particularly effective for aging datasets where local pulse-response features (captured by CNN) must be interpreted in the context of the cell's long-term degradation history (captured by LSTM).


\section{Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)}
\label{sec:pinns}

Purely data-driven neural networks can generate physically implausible predictionsâ€”such as negative internal resistances or non-monotonic capacity decayâ€”when exposed to noisy sensor data or out-of-distribution driving conditions. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) resolve this by embedding governing differential equations directly into the loss function.

Consider a multi-layer perceptron $\hat{V}_t(t; \mathbf{w}, b)$ trained to predict terminal voltage under load current $I(t)$ while simultaneously extracting physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = [R_0, R_1, C_1]^T$.

The overall PINN loss function $\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}}$ combines data fitting terms with physical residual penalties:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{w}, b) + \lambda_{\text{phys}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \lambda_{\text{bound}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{bound}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
\label{eq:ch8_pinn_loss_total}
\end{equation}

The data loss term penalizes terminal voltage prediction errors across $N_d$ discrete measurement points:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{w}, b) = \frac{1}{N_d} \sum_{i=1}^{N_d} \left| V_{t,\text{meas}}(t_i) - \hat{V}_t(t_i; \mathbf{w}, b) \right|^2
\label{eq:ch8_pinn_data_loss}
\end{equation}

The physics residual loss evaluates compliance with governing differential equations across $N_p$ collocation points using Automatic Differentiation (AD):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} \left| \frac{d\hat{V}_1}{dt}(t_j) + \frac{1}{R_1 C_1} \hat{V}_1(t_j) - \frac{1}{C_1} I(t_j) \right|^2
\label{eq:ch8_pinn_physics_loss}
\end{equation}

Boundary penalty terms enforce thermodynamic parameter guardrails:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{bound}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \max(0, -R_0)^2 + \max(0, -R_1)^2 + \max(0, -C_1)^2
\label{eq:ch8_pinn_bound_loss}
\end{equation}

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}}$ via backpropagation enforces physical consistency while reducing required experimental training data by up to an order of magnitude compared to purely data-driven networks.


\subsection{Automatic Differentiation and Collocation Point Selection}
The physics residual term in Equation~\eqref{eq:ch8_pinn_physics_loss} requires evaluating the time derivative $d\hat{V}_1/dt$ of the neural network output. This is computed efficiently using Automatic Differentiation (AD), which tracks the computational graph of forward-pass operations and applies the chain rule analytically. For a neural network with $L$ layers and ReLU activations:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\hat{V}_1}{dt} = \frac{\partial \hat{V}_1}{\partial \mathbf{h}_L} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{L-1}} \cdots \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_1}{\partial t}
\end{equation}
This computation has the same cost as the forward pass (one backpropagation pass), making PINN training only $\sim 2\times$ slower than standard neural network training for the same network size.

Collocation points $\{t_j\}_{j=1}^{N_p}$ are selected to uniformly cover the physical domain of interest (the discharge time window $[0, T_{\text{discharge}}]$). Two strategies are commonly used:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uniform Collocation:} $N_p$ evenly spaced points across $[0, T_{\text{discharge}}]$. Simple but may under-represent rapidly changing regions (e.g., pulse transitions).
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Collocation:} Additional points are placed in regions of high physics residual $\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}(t_j)$, concentrating computational resources on poorly satisfied physics constraints.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{PINN Architecture for Multi-Parameter Estimation}
For simultaneous extraction of all three ECM parameters $[R_0, R_1, C_1]^T$, a structured PINN is designed:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A shared trunk network $\mathcal{N}_{\text{trunk}}(\mathbf{x}_t; \mathbf{w}_{\text{trunk}})$ processes the input features $\mathbf{x}_t = [I(t), V_t(t), T(t)]^T$ and produces a shared feature embedding $\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H}$.
    \item Three separate branch heads $\mathcal{N}_{R_0}$, $\mathcal{N}_{R_1}$, $\mathcal{N}_{C_1}$ map the shared embedding to individual parameter estimates:
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{R}_{0,t} = \text{softplus}\left( \mathcal{N}_{R_0}(\mathbf{z}_t) \right), \quad \hat{R}_{1,t} = \text{softplus}\left( \mathcal{N}_{R_1}(\mathbf{z}_t) \right), \quad \hat{C}_{1,t} = \text{softplus}\left( \mathcal{N}_{C_1}(\mathbf{z}_t) \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{softplus}(x) = \ln(1 + e^x) > 0$ automatically enforces the thermodynamic positivity constraint $R_0, R_1, C_1 > 0$, eliminating the need for the explicit boundary loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{bound}}$.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Transfer Learning for Cross-Chemistry Adaptation}
A key limitation of purely data-driven models is that a model trained on one battery chemistry (e.g., $\text{LiFePO}_4$) cannot be directly applied to a different chemistry (e.g., NMC) without full retraining. Transfer Learning resolves this by reusing the knowledge encoded in a pre-trained model and fine-tuning it with a small amount of target chemistry data.

The transfer learning workflow for battery parameter estimation proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-training on Source Chemistry:} Train the full PINN (trunk + branch heads) on a large dataset from the source chemistry (e.g., 1000 discharge cycles of $\text{LiFePO}_4$).
    \item \textbf{Feature Extractor Freezing:} Freeze the weights of the trunk network $\mathcal{N}_{\text{trunk}}$ (which encodes physics-informed temporal features). Only the branch head weights are updated during fine-tuning.
    \item \textbf{Fine-Tuning on Target Chemistry:} Train only the branch heads on a small target chemistry dataset (e.g., 50 discharge cycles of NMC). The frozen trunk provides a strong physical prior, enabling accurate NMC parameter estimation with minimal data.
\end{enumerate}

This approach reduces the required training data for a new chemistry by $95\%$ compared to training from scratch, making it practical for BMS deployment on new cell formats.


\section{Cloud-Based Architectures and Edge Computing}
\label{sec:cloud_edge}

Deploying resource-intensive deep learning models on embedded automotive microcontrollers (Edge BMS) requires balancing local processing limits against cloud analytical capacity. Cloud-edge collaborative BMS architectures split computation across two layers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Edge Layer (On-Vehicle BMS):} Executes lightweight inference models (e.g., 1D-CNN, pruned SVR) at high sampling rates ($10\,\text{Hz} - 100\,\text{Hz}$) for real-time state tracking and fault detection.
    \item \textbf{Cloud Layer (Centralized Platform):} Pools telematics data across vehicle fleets ($10^3 - 10^5$ battery packs), training heavy PINNs and GPR models using high-performance GPU clusters, delivering updated parameter lookup tables via Over-The-Air (OTA) firmware updates.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Model Compression and Quantization for Edge Deployment}
Deep learning models trained in the cloud (using full 32-bit floating-point precision) must be compressed and quantized for deployment on resource-constrained microcontrollers. Three key compression techniques are used:

\subsubsection{Weight Pruning}
Weight pruning removes redundant neural network connections by setting weights below a magnitude threshold $\tau_p$ to zero:
\begin{equation}
W_{ij}^{\text{pruned}} = \begin{cases} W_{ij} & \text{if } |W_{ij}| \ge \tau_p \\ 0 & \text{if } |W_{ij}| < \tau_p \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Iterative magnitude pruning achieves a compression ratio of $80\%$--$95\%$ (removing 80--95\% of weights) with less than $1\%$ degradation in parameter estimation accuracy, reducing the model's memory footprint from MBs to tens of KBs.

\subsubsection{Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)}
Post-Training Quantization converts 32-bit floating-point weights and activations to 8-bit integer representation:
\begin{equation}
W_{\text{int8}} = \text{round}\left( \frac{W_{\text{float32}}}{\Delta} \right), \quad \Delta = \frac{W_{\max} - W_{\min}}{2^8 - 1}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta$ is the quantization scale factor. INT8 quantization reduces model memory by $4\times$ and enables computation using integer arithmetic units (which are $3$--$4\times$ faster than floating-point units on typical ARM Cortex-M microcontrollers).

\subsubsection{Knowledge Distillation}
In knowledge distillation, a large, accurate teacher model $f_{\text{teacher}}(\mathbf{x})$ supervises the training of a small, efficient student model $f_{\text{student}}(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_S) = (1 - \alpha) \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_S) + \alpha \cdot T^2 \cdot \text{KL}\left[ \sigma\!\left(\frac{f_{\text{teacher}}}{T}\right) \,\bigg\|\, \sigma\!\left(\frac{f_{\text{student}}}{T}\right) \right]
\end{equation}
where $T > 1$ is the temperature parameter softening the teacher's output distribution, $\alpha \in [0,1]$ balances the task loss against the distillation loss, and $\text{KL}[\cdot \| \cdot]$ is the KL divergence. Knowledge distillation enables a 5-layer LSTM student (100 parameters) to match the accuracy of a 20-layer BiLSTM teacher (2000 parameters) on $R_0$ estimation.


\subsection{Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Fleet Parameter Estimation}
In a fleet of electric vehicles, battery data contains sensitive information about driver behavior, travel routes, and charging habits. Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across $K$ vehicles without sharing raw data:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Global Model Broadcast:} The cloud server broadcasts the current global model weights $\mathbf{W}^{(r)}$ to all $K$ vehicles at communication round $r$.
    \item \textbf{Local Training:} Each vehicle $k$ trains the model on its local battery dataset $\mathcal{D}_k$ for $E$ local epochs, producing a locally updated model $\mathbf{W}_k^{(r+1)}$.
    \item \textbf{Gradient Aggregation:} The server aggregates the local model updates using weighted averaging (FedAvg algorithm):
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W}^{(r+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{|\mathcal{D}_k|}{\sum_j |\mathcal{D}_j|} \mathbf{W}_k^{(r+1)}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Privacy Guarantee:} Since only model weight updates (not raw measurements) are shared, individual vehicle data never leaves the local BMS. Differential privacy can be added by clipping gradients and injecting calibrated Gaussian noise before aggregation.
\end{enumerate}

Federated learning enables battery parameter models to benefit from fleet-scale diversity (different climate zones, driving styles, and charging behaviors) while satisfying GDPR and automotive data privacy regulations.

\subsection{Online Model Adaptation (OTA Updates)}
Once cloud-trained parameter models are deployed to the vehicle fleet, they must remain accurate as cells age. An Over-The-Air (OTA) update cycle is established:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drift Detection:} The on-vehicle BMS monitors the residual between the AI-estimated parameter ($\hat{R}_0^{\text{AI}}$) and the physics-based estimator ($\hat{R}_0^{\text{EKF}}$). When the normalized residual exceeds a threshold $\delta_{\text{drift}}$ for more than $N_{\text{drift}}$ consecutive cycles, a re-training request is flagged.
    \item \textbf{Cloud Re-training:} The cloud platform aggregates fleet data from similar aged cells, fine-tunes the deployed model, and packages a compressed quantized update.
    \item \textbf{OTA Deployment:} The updated model is deployed to the vehicle via the LTE/5G telematics unit during a scheduled charging session.
\end{itemize}


\section{Attention Mechanisms and Transformer-Based Estimation}
\label{sec:attention_transformers}

Recurrent architectures (LSTM, GRU) process sequences step-by-step, which limits parallelization during training and makes it difficult to capture long-range dependencies without a large number of stacked layers. The Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), resolves this by replacing recurrence with self-attention mechanisms that model all pairwise interactions between sequence positions simultaneously.

\subsection{Scaled Dot-Product Self-Attention}
Given a sequence of $T$ feature vectors (one per time step) stacked into a matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$, the scaled dot-product self-attention computes queries $\mathbf{Q}$, keys $\mathbf{K}$, and values $\mathbf{V}$ as linear projections:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X} \mathbf{W}_Q, \quad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X} \mathbf{W}_K, \quad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X} \mathbf{W}_V
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}$ are learnable projection matrices.

The attention output is computed as a weighted sum of values, where the weights are determined by the softmax of the scaled query-key dot products:
\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \mathbf{V}
\label{eq:ch8_scaled_attn}
\end{equation}
where $\sqrt{d_k}$ is a scaling factor that prevents gradient vanishing when $d_k$ is large.

The attention weight matrix $\mathbf{A} = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times T}$ can be interpreted as a learned pairwise correlation matrix between all pairs of time steps in the battery measurement sequence. For battery parameter estimation, the attention mechanism learns to focus on the most informative time stepsâ€”specifically, the moments of current pulse transitions and voltage relaxation transientsâ€”that contain the most information about internal resistance parameters.

\subsection{Multi-Head Attention}
Multi-head attention runs $h$ parallel attention heads, each with separate projection matrices, then concatenates and linearly projects their outputs:
\begin{align}
\text{head}_i &= \text{Attention}\!\left( \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{Q,i}, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{K,i}, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{V,i} \right) \\
\text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X}) &= \text{Concat}[\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h] \mathbf{W}_O
\end{align}
Different heads can learn to attend to different aspects of the signalâ€”one head may focus on high-frequency voltage steps (encoding $R_0$), while another attends to slow exponential relaxation tails (encoding $R_1 C_1$).

\subsection{Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) for Battery State Estimation}
The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT), proposed by Lim et al. (2021), extends the standard Transformer with gating mechanisms and variable selection networks tailored for multi-variate time series with mixed-frequency inputs. In the battery context, TFT simultaneously ingests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Past observed inputs:} Current $I_t$, voltage $V_{t,t}$, temperature $T_t$ (measured at every time step).
    \item \textbf{Known future inputs:} Planned charge/discharge protocol (if available).
    \item \textbf{Static metadata:} Cell chemistry, nominal capacity, manufacturing date.
\end{itemize}
The TFT's variable selection network assigns learnable importance weights to each input feature, automatically identifying which measurements are most informative for parameter estimation at each time step.

\section{Bayesian Neural Networks for Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:bayesian_nns}

Standard deep learning models produce point estimates $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ without quantifying the uncertainty in those estimates. In safety-critical BMS applications, under-estimated resistance values ($\hat{R}_0 < R_0^{\text{true}}$) can lead to overly optimistic power delivery estimates, causing thermal runaway. Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) address this by treating the network weights as probability distributions rather than deterministic values, propagating uncertainty through the model.

\subsection{Variational Inference for BNNs}
In a BNN, the weight distributions $p(\mathbf{W})$ are learned via Variational Inference (VI). A parametric variational distribution $q(\mathbf{W} | \boldsymbol{\lambda})$ (typically Gaussian: $q(\mathbf{W} | \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2))$) is fitted to the intractable true posterior $p(\mathbf{W} | \mathcal{D})$ by minimizing the KL divergence:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BNN}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{W}|\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\left[ -\log p(\mathcal{D} | \mathbf{W}) \right] + \text{KL}\left[ q(\mathbf{W} | \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \| p(\mathbf{W}) \right]
\label{eq:ch8_bnn_elbo}
\end{equation}
The first term is the negative log-likelihood (reconstruction loss) and the second term is the KL divergence from the prior $p(\mathbf{W})$ (typically $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$), which acts as a regularizer.

\subsection{Practical Dropout as Approximate BNN}
Monte Carlo Dropout (MC-Dropout) provides a practical approximation to BNN inference without modifying the standard training procedure. During both training and inference, dropout masks are applied randomly to network activations. At test time, $T$ stochastic forward passes are performed with different dropout masks, generating $T$ parameter estimates:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)} = f_{\text{dropout}}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{W}, \mathbf{m}^{(t)}), \quad t = 1, \dots, T
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{m}^{(t)}$ is a random binary dropout mask drawn at each forward pass.

The mean and variance of these $T$ samples approximate the predictive mean and epistemic uncertainty:
\begin{align}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{mean}} &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)} \\
\sigma^2_{\text{epistemic}} &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{mean}} \right\|^2
\end{align}
A BMS controller can use $\sigma^2_{\text{epistemic}}$ to flag high-uncertainty parameter estimates (e.g., during cold-start at $-10^\circ\text{C}$ where training data is sparse) and fall back to the physics-based estimator (EKF) until confidence improves.


\subsection{Conformal Prediction for Distribution-Free Uncertainty}
Conformal Prediction provides a distribution-free, statistically valid uncertainty quantification framework that requires no assumptions about the noise distribution. Given a calibration dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{cal}}}$ and a nominal coverage level $1 - \alpha$ (e.g., $90\%$), the conformal prediction interval for a new test point $\mathbf{x}^*$ is constructed as:
\begin{equation}
\hat{C}_\alpha(\mathbf{x}^*) = \left[ \hat{y}(\mathbf{x}^*) - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}, \quad \hat{y}(\mathbf{x}^*) + \hat{q}_{1-\alpha} \right]
\label{eq:ch8_conformal_pi}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}$ is the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the non-conformity scores $\{|y_i - \hat{y}(\mathbf{x}_i)|\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{cal}}}$ computed on the calibration set. This interval is guaranteed to contain the true parameter value $y^*$ with probability at least $1 - \alpha$ regardless of the underlying data distribution, providing a rigorous safety certificate for BMS deployment.


\section{Reservoir Computing for Embedded Battery Estimation}
\label{sec:reservoir_computing}

Reservoir Computing (RC), specifically Echo State Networks (ESN), provides an extremely computationally efficient approach to sequence modelling for embedded BMS deployment. Unlike LSTM, which trains all weights via backpropagation-through-time (BPTT), ESN fixes the randomly initialized recurrent weights (the ``reservoir'') and only trains the linear output layer. This reduces training complexity from $\mathcal{O}(T \cdot H^2)$ (LSTM BPTT) to $\mathcal{O}(H^2)$ (single pseudoinverse solve), enabling training directly on a microcontroller without cloud infrastructure.

\subsection{Echo State Network Structure}
An ESN consists of three components:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input layer:} A fixed random weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d}$ projects the input vector $\mathbf{x}_t = [I_t, V_{t,t}, T_t]^T$ into the reservoir.
    \item \textbf{Reservoir layer:} A fixed random recurrent weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_{\text{res}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times H}$ (scaled to have spectral radius $\rho(\mathbf{W}_{\text{res}}) < 1$ to ensure the echo state property) drives the reservoir state dynamics:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}_t = \tanh\left( \mathbf{W}_{\text{in}} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{\text{res}} \mathbf{r}_{t-1} \right)
    \label{eq:ch8_esn_state}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Output layer:} A trainable linear readout weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\theta \times H}$ maps reservoir states to parameter estimates:
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t = \mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} \mathbf{r}_t
    \label{eq:ch8_esn_output}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The output weights $\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}$ are computed offline via ridge regression on the training set $\{(\mathbf{r}_t, \boldsymbol{\theta}_t)\}_{t=1}^N$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} = \boldsymbol{\Theta} \mathbf{R}^T \left( \mathbf{R} \mathbf{R}^T + \lambda_r \mathbf{I} \right)^{-1}
\label{eq:ch8_esn_ridge}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R} = [\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N] \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times N}$, $\boldsymbol{\Theta} = [\boldsymbol{\theta}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\theta}_N] \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\theta \times N}$, and $\lambda_r > 0$ is the ridge regularization constant.

This single ridge regression solve is extremely fast ($<1\,\text{ms}$ for $H = 500$, $N = 10000$) and can be performed entirely on a 32-bit microcontroller, enabling on-device model calibration without cloud connectivity.

    \item \textbf{Deep Learning (LSTM/1D-CNN):} Capture complex temporal dynamic signatures across long drive cycles; 1D-CNN and TCN provide parallelized low-latency inference.
    \item \textbf{Physics-Informed NNs (PINNs):} Bridge empirical data and physical laws by embedding differential models directly into training loss functions, ensuring robust parameter tracking with lower training data requirements.
    \item \textbf{Transfer Learning and Federated Learning:} Enable cross-chemistry adaptation and privacy-preserving fleet-scale model training, dramatically reducing the cost of deploying AI-based BMS across diverse vehicle populations.
\end{itemize}

The broader conclusion of this chapter is that data-driven estimation is most effective when it is used as a complement to physical understanding rather than as a replacement for it. The strongest battery estimation frameworks combine mechanistic insight with machine learning flexibility: the physical model informs the operating constraints, while the learning model captures nonlinearities and degradation signatures that are difficult to encode analytically. This hybrid philosophy naturally leads to the final estimation paradigm explored in Chapter~9, where stochastic-gradient methods are used to achieve low-cost online adaptation with embedded hardware constraints in mind.
