\section{Overview}
\label{sec:overview_lib}

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) dominate modern energy storage architectures, ranging from portable micro-electronics to utility-scale grid stabilization systems and electric vehicle (EV) powertrains. This ubiquity is driven by their superior specific energy (150--300 Wh/kg), high nominal cell voltage (3.2--3.8~V depending on cathode chemistry), and low self-discharge rates.

\subsection{Electrochemical Operating Principles}

The fundamental operation of an LIB relies on the reversible intercalation and de-intercalation of lithium ions between two host structures. A conventional cell consists of a transition metal oxide cathode (e.g., $\mathrm{LiCoO_2}$, $\mathrm{LiFePO_4}$, or $\mathrm{LiNi_xMn_yCo_zO_2}$), a graphitic anode ($\mathrm{C_6}$), a micro-porous separator, and a non-aqueous electrolyte based on $\mathrm{LiPF_6}$ dissolved in organic carbonates.

During charging, an external source drives electrons from the cathode to the anode while lithium ions migrate through the electrolyte and intercalate into the anode. For a standard $\mathrm{LiCoO_2}$ (LCO) cell:

\noindent\textbf{Positive electrode:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cathode_rxn}
\mathrm{LiCoO_2} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{Li_{1-x}CoO_2} + x\mathrm{Li^+} + x\mathrm{e^-}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Negative electrode:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:anode_rxn}
\mathrm{C_6} + x\mathrm{Li^+} + x\mathrm{e^-} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{Li_xC_6}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Overall reaction:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overall_rxn}
\mathrm{LiCoO_2} + \mathrm{C_6} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{Li_{1-x}CoO_2} + \mathrm{Li_xC_6}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Thermodynamic and Kinetic Foundations}

The terminal voltage of a cell is governed by underlying thermodynamic and kinetic phenomena. The equilibrium potential, or Open Circuit Voltage (OCV), depends on the local lithium concentration at the electrode surfaces and on temperature, as described by the Nernst equation:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nernst}
E_{eq} = E^0 - \frac{RT}{nF} \ln\!\left(\frac{a_{red}}{a_{ox}}\right)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $E^0$ is the standard electrode potential, $R$ is the universal gas constant, $T$ is absolute temperature, $n$ is the number of transferred electrons, $F$ is the Faraday constant, and $a_{red}, a_{ox}$ are the activities of the reduced and oxidized species.

When current flows, the terminal voltage deviates from the OCV due to ohmic, activation, and concentration overpotentials. Charge-transfer kinetics at the electrode--electrolyte interface follow the Butler--Volmer equation:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:butler_volmer}
j = j_0 \left[ \exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha_a n F \eta_{act}}{RT}\right) - \exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha_c n F \eta_{act}}{RT}\right) \right]
\end{equation}

\noindent where $j$ is the current density, $j_0$ is the exchange current density, and $\alpha_a,\alpha_c$ are the anodic and cathodic transfer coefficients \cite{newman1993lithium}.

Because these parameters are non-linear, time-variant, and thermally coupled, static lookup tables are insufficient for modern control strategies.

\section{The Role of the Battery Management System (BMS)}
\label{sec:bms_role}

A Battery Management System (BMS) is a cyber-physical system that ensures the safe, efficient, and extended operation of a battery pack. Modern EV packs contain hundreds or thousands of cells in series and parallel; manufacturing tolerances and thermal gradients cause these cells to behave non-uniformly over time, requiring active management \cite{lu2013review}.

\subsection{BMS Architecture}

An Analog Front-End (AFE) performs high-frequency sampling of cell voltages, module temperatures, and pack current. This telemetry is transmitted--typically via isoSPI or CAN--to the main Microcontroller Unit (MCU) for processing.

\subsection{Distinguishing State and Parameter Estimation}

The MCU's algorithmic layer must mathematically decouple state estimation from parameter estimation, as the two operate on different time scales and serve different objectives \cite{plett2004extended}. Consider a discrete-time nonlinear state-space model:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:state_space_bms}
\begin{aligned}
x_{k+1} &= f(x_k, u_k, \theta_k) + w_k \\
y_k &= h(x_k, u_k, \theta_k) + v_k
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $u_k$ is the input vector (e.g., current $I_k$, ambient temperature $T_{amb}$), $y_k$ is the measured output (e.g., terminal voltage $V_t$), and $w_k, v_k$ are zero-mean process and measurement noise.

\textbf{State estimation ($x_k$):} contains variables that evolve rapidly, such as State of Charge (SOC) and internal polarization voltages, changing over seconds to minutes in response to $u_k$.

\textbf{Parameter estimation ($\theta_k$):} contains coefficients defining $f(\cdot)$ and $h(\cdot)$, such as capacity $Q$, ohmic resistance $R_0$, and polarization time constants $\tau$. These evolve slowly, over months or years, due to degradation.

The central thesis of this report is that accurate state estimation is not possible if the parameter vector $\theta_k$ is outdated. Advanced BMS architectures therefore require continuous \emph{online parameter identification} to keep the state-space model consistent with the physical plant.

\section{The Need for Parameter Estimation}
\label{sec:need_parameter_estimation}

Lithium-ion cells are volatile electrochemical systems whose internal characteristics shift continuously. Relying solely on parameters calibrated at Beginning-of-Life (BOL) causes predictive accuracy to diverge from physical reality as the cell ages.

\subsection{The Challenge of Time-Variant Dynamics}

The internal resistance $R_0$, for example, is a function of several operating conditions:
\begin{equation}
R_0 = f(\text{SOC}, T, \text{current direction}, \text{SOH})
\end{equation}

During a high-current pulse, $R_0$ drops momentarily due to self-heating; at low temperature and low SOC, diffusion limitations sharply increase apparent impedance. A fixed $R_0$ therefore produces inaccurate voltage-drop estimates ($V = I R_0$), leading to false over-/under-voltage alarms and unnecessary safety disconnects \cite{hu2012comparative}.

Parameter identifiability is also condition-dependent: the current profile must be sufficiently rich in frequency content to excite all dynamic modes. Under steady constant-current charging, polarization parameters become weakly observable, complicating estimation.

\subsection{Safety, Aging, and Reliability}
\label{subsec:safety_aging}

Real-time parameter estimation is essential for functional safety and degradation diagnosis. Battery aging is broadly categorized as cycle aging (mechanical stress during charge/discharge) and calendar aging (thermodynamic instability over time, even at rest).

\subsubsection{Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) Growth}

The dominant degradation mechanism in graphitic anodes is the continuous thickening of the Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) layer, formed during initial cycling as electrolyte solvent reduces at the anode surface. While the layer passivates the anode, it also consumes cyclable lithium and increases resistance \cite{vetter2005aging}. Its growth is often modeled as diffusion-limited:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sei_growth}
\frac{d\delta_{SEI}}{dt} = \frac{M_{SEI}}{\rho_{SEI} F}\, j_s
\end{equation}

\noindent where $M_{SEI}$, $\rho_{SEI}$ are the molecular weight and density of the SEI products, and $j_s$ is the parasitic side-reaction current density. Macroscopically, this manifests as a shift in the parameter vector: capacity $Q$ decreases and resistance $R_0$ increases -- an indirect but accurate signature of internal degradation.

\subsubsection{Thermal Modeling and Runaway Prevention}

Heat generation inside a cell follows the Bernardi equation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bernardi}
\dot{q} = I^2 R_{internal} + I\, T_{cell}\, \frac{\partial U}{\partial T}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the first term is irreversible Joule heating and the second is reversible entropic heating ($U$ is the OCV). The lumped-capacitance thermal model of a cell is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:thermal_mass}
m c_p \frac{dT_{cell}}{dt} = \dot{q} - hA\,(T_{cell} - T_{amb})
\end{equation}

\noindent where $m$ is mass, $c_p$ is specific heat capacity, $h$ is the convective heat transfer coefficient, and $A$ is surface area. An outdated $R_{internal}$ causes the BMS to underestimate $dT_{cell}/dt$ -- a serious risk, since exothermic decomposition (SEI breakdown near $90^\circ$C, separator melting near $130^\circ$C, cathode oxygen release) follows Arrhenius kinetics. Early detection of rising resistance via recursive parameter estimation allows power derating before critical thermal thresholds are reached.

\subsubsection{State of Health (SOH) Tracking}

Parameter identification directly yields the metrics needed for SOH calculation, split into capacity SOH and power SOH:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sohq}
\text{SOH}_Q = \frac{\hat{Q}_{current}}{Q_{nominal}} \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sohr}
\text{SOH}_R = \frac{R_{EOL} - \hat{R}_{current}}{R_{EOL} - R_{BOL}} \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $\hat{Q}_{current}$ and $\hat{R}_{current}$ are outputs of online estimation algorithms (e.g., Recursive Least Squares or Dual Extended Kalman Filters). Tracking these values prevents unexpected capacity drop-offs and signals when a battery must be retired for second-life use.

\subsection{Performance Optimization}
\label{subsec:performance_opt}

Beyond safety, commercial viability requires extracting maximum performance from the pack, which means operating cells close to their electrochemical limits without exceeding them.

\subsubsection{Dynamic State of Power (SOP) Estimation}

State of Power (SOP) defines the maximum current or power a battery can sustain over a prediction horizon $\Delta t$ (e.g., 10~s) without violating voltage or SOC limits. Static designs compute peak discharge current with a simple algebraic constraint:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sop_static}
I_{max}^{dis} = \frac{\text{OCV}(SOC) - V_{min}}{R_{static}}
\end{equation}

Because $R_{static}$ must cover worst-case aging and temperature conditions, this approach unnecessarily limits power output, throttling acceleration and wasting regenerative braking energy.

With online parameter estimation, the BMS instead tracks the transient impedance parameters (polarization resistances $R_{p1}, R_{p2}$ and capacitances $C_{p1}, C_{p2}$) and predicts the voltage response to a hypothetical current pulse $I$:

\begin{align}
\label{eq:sop_dynamic}
V(t+\Delta t) \;=\; &\;\text{OCV}\big(SOC(t+\Delta t)\big) \;-\; I R_0 \notag \\
&-\; I R_{p1}\left(1 - e^{-\Delta t / (R_{p1}C_{p1})}\right)
\end{align}

Setting $V(t+\Delta t)$ to the minimum safe voltage $V_{min}$ allows the BMS to solve for the true maximum sustainable current $I_{max}^{dis}$ \cite{sun2015state}.

\subsubsection{Fast Charging Strategies}

Fast charging in modern 800V EV architectures depends on dynamic parameter awareness. High C-rate charging risks lithium plating -- lithium depositing as solid metal rather than intercalating -- a phenomenon governed by diffusion time constants that vary with temperature and SOC. By co-estimating diffusion parameters alongside SOC, Model Predictive Control (MPC) can shape the charging current: injecting high current when diffusion pathways are clear, and throttling before anodic overpotential crosses the plating threshold. This shifts fast charging from conservative, step-wise constant-current profiles toward dynamic, optimal-time control.



\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary_ch1}

This chapter established the motivation for studying parameter estimation in lithium-ion BMS. The electrochemical fundamentals -- intercalation kinetics and the Nernstian voltage response -- show that terminal behavior is inherently non-linear and variable over the battery lifecycle.

The BMS architecture was defined, with a clear mathematical separation between rapidly changing states (e.g., SOC) and slowly varying parameters (e.g., resistance, capacity). Analysis of thermal runaway dynamics and SEI growth showed that static parameter models pose safety risks and force overly conservative operating limits. Dynamic parameter estimation is therefore essential for accurate SOH tracking and for maximizing SOP during aggressive operation. The following chapters formulate, compare, and validate the algorithms capable of meeting these real-time requirements.
