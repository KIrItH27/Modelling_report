\section{Introduction to Lab Profiling}
\label{sec:lab_profiling}

Chapter 2 established the model structures -- equivalent circuit models (ECMs), pseudo-two-dimensional (P2D) models, and single particle models (SPM) -- upon which the parameter estimation methods of Chapters 4 through 9 are built. Every estimation method requires measured data: current, voltage, and temperature records collected under controlled excitation. This chapter details the laboratory procedures that produce this data, bridging theory with physical application.

\subsection{Purpose and Scope}

Laboratory profiling applies controlled current, voltage, or small-signal perturbations to a cell under defined environmental conditions to extract model parameters. Four core procedures are developed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OCV--SOC characterization} (\S\ref{sec:ocv_soc}): establishes the equilibrium open-circuit voltage function $V_{oc}(z,T)$.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization (HPPC)} (\S\ref{sec:hppc}): the primary source for transient ECM parameters ($R_0, R_1, C_1$).
    \item \textbf{Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS)} (\S\ref{sec:eis}): high-resolution frequency-domain characterization of internal impedance.
    \item \textbf{Sensor noise characterization} (\S\ref{sec:noise_preprocessing}): pre-processing steps that convert raw sensor data into estimation-ready signals.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Test Equipment and Environmental Control}

Three instrumentation constraints apply to all tests: (1) \textbf{Temperature control} -- tests must run inside an environmental chamber at thermal equilibrium, since all parameters are temperature-dependent; (2) \textbf{Cycler/potentiostat capability} -- HPPC and OCV tests need a programmable cycler, while EIS needs a precision potentiostat capable of small-amplitude sinusoidal perturbations; (3) \textbf{Fixturing} -- four-wire (Kelvin) sensing is mandatory to eliminate voltage drop across current-carrying leads.

\section{Open Circuit Voltage (OCV) vs.\ SOC Relationship}
\label{sec:ocv_soc}

The true open-circuit voltage ($V_{oc}$) is the terminal voltage measured after the cell reaches full electrochemical equilibrium, at which point ohmic, charge-transfer, and diffusion overpotentials have all decayed to zero.

Since true equilibrium relaxation requires hours per SOC point, two practical approximations are used. The \textbf{low-current (quasi-equilibrium) method} discharges or charges the cell at a near-constant, very low C-rate (e.g., C/30); overpotentials are assumed negligible, so terminal voltage approximates $V_{oc}(z)$ continuously. The \textbf{incremental (pulse-and-rest) method} instead discharges the cell in discrete steps (e.g., 5\% SOC increments), each followed by a multi-hour rest to allow relaxation; this is far slower but gives reference-quality accuracy.

OCV is also path-dependent: the voltage measured after charging differs from that measured after discharging at the same SOC, an effect known as \textbf{hysteresis}, which can exceed 150~mV in silicon-containing anodes. In practice, the low-current procedure is run in both directions to obtain $V_{oc}^{\text{chg}}(z)$ and $V_{oc}^{\text{dis}}(z)$. Standard ECM applications typically average the two curves; high-fidelity applications (e.g., EV regenerative braking) instead append a dedicated hysteresis state to the model.

\section{Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization\\ (HPPC)}
\label{sec:hppc}

The HPPC test extracts a cell's dynamic power capability and transient ECM parameters. At each targeted SOC set-point: (1) a constant-current discharge pulse (e.g., 10~s) is applied while voltage is logged at high rate; (2) the cell rests (e.g., 3~min) until fast transients decay; (3) a regenerative charge pulse (e.g., 20~s) is applied; (4) a second rest period follows; (5) the cell is discharged to the next SOC set-point (e.g., 10\% lower) and allowed to reach thermal equilibrium before the sequence repeats.

\section{Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS)}
\label{sec:eis}

While HPPC operates in the time domain, EIS characterizes impedance in the frequency domain. A small-amplitude sinusoidal current $i(t) = I_0 \sin(\omega t)$ is swept across a wide frequency range (e.g., mHz to kHz); the phase-shifted voltage response yields the complex impedance $Z(\omega)$ without pushing the cell outside its linear operating regime.

EIS data is visualized on a Nyquist plot ($-Z_{\text{im}}$ vs.\ $Z_{\text{re}}$) and interpreted using a Randles equivalent circuit. A typical spectrum shows a high-frequency real-axis intercept (series resistance $R_s$), a mid-frequency semicircle (charge-transfer resistance $R_{ct}$ and double-layer capacitance $C_{dl}$), and a low-frequency 45$^\circ$ tail corresponding to Warburg solid-state diffusion ($Z_W$). EIS provides a high-resolution cross-check for time-domain parameters, particularly useful for separating closely coupled fast-polarization time constants.

\section{Sensor Noise and Data Pre-processing}
\label{sec:noise_preprocessing}

Raw BMS data is susceptible to electromagnetic interference (EMI), finite sensor resolution, and synchronization artifacts. Before parameter identification, raw data is pre-processed in three steps: \textbf{low-pass filtering} attenuates high-frequency EMI, with the cutoff chosen conservatively to avoid suppressing genuine fast-transient RC dynamics; \textbf{outlier rejection} removes isolated sensor glitches via thresholding or median filtering; and \textbf{resampling} synchronizes current, voltage, and temperature onto a common, uniform time base required for Coulomb counting.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary_ch3}

This chapter detailed the experimental procedures needed to populate the model structures introduced in Chapter 2. OCV tests yield the thermodynamic voltage bounds, HPPC pulse profiles provide transient real-time ECM parameters, and EIS cross-verifies these metrics in the frequency domain. Because data fidelity directly determines estimation accuracy, proper signal filtering and hysteresis management are mandatory prerequisites for the offline regression algorithms developed in Chapter 4.
