\label{chap:modeling}

\section{Introduction to Modeling Approaches}
\label{sec:intro_modeling}

Parameter estimation requires a defined mathematical model structure. Lithium-ion battery models fall broadly into three families, trading physical fidelity against computational cost:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Equivalent circuit models (ECMs)}: represent terminal behavior using resistors, capacitors, and a voltage source. Parameters are empirical but lightweight, making ECMs standard for BMS use.
    \item \textbf{Electrochemical models}: describe lithium transport and charge transfer through coupled partial differential equations (PDEs), offering high fidelity at high computational cost.
    \item \textbf{Thermal-coupled extensions}: add an energy balance to either family to capture temperature-dependent behavior.
\end{enumerate}

This report separates \textbf{parameter estimation} (identifying structural constants such as $R_0$ or $D_s$) from \textbf{state estimation} (tracking dynamic variables such as SOC or temperature). Both rely on the model families below.

Unless stated otherwise, models in this report assume: a single cell, positive current $I(t)$ during discharge, volume-averaged electrode structures, and negligible parasitic side reactions under nominal operation.

\section{Equivalent Circuit Models}
\label{sec:ecm}

All ECM topologies share the same general terminal-voltage relationship:
\begin{equation}
    V_t(t) = V_{oc}\big(z(t), T\big) - \sum_{i} \eta_i(t) - I(t)\, R_0,
    \label{eq:generic_ecm}
\end{equation}
where $V_t$ is terminal voltage, $V_{oc}(z,T)$ is open-circuit voltage as a function of state of charge $z$ and temperature $T$, $R_0$ is instantaneous ohmic resistance, and $\eta_i$ is the polarization overpotential of the $i$-th RC branch. State of charge follows Coulomb counting in every ECM:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz(t)}{dt} = -\frac{\eta_c\, I(t)}{Q_n},
    \label{eq:coulomb_counting}
\end{equation}
where $Q_n$ is nominal capacity and $\eta_c$ is Coulombic efficiency.

\textbf{Rint model.} The simplest case represents the cell as an OCV source in series with a single resistance, with no transient memory:
\begin{equation}
    V_t(t) = V_{oc}\big(z(t)\big) - I(t)\,R_0.
    \label{eq:rint}
\end{equation}
It is computationally minimal but underpredicts voltage relaxation after a current step.

\textbf{Thevenin (first-order) model.} Adding one parallel RC branch $(R_1, C_1)$ captures exponential voltage relaxation:
\begin{equation}
    V_t(t) = V_{oc}\big(z(t)\big) - U_p(t) - I(t)\,R_0,
    \label{eq:thevenin_terminal}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dU_p(t)}{dt} = -\frac{U_p(t)}{R_1 C_1} + \frac{I(t)}{C_1},
    \label{eq:thevenin_rc}
\end{equation}
where the polarization voltage $U_p$ relaxes with time constant $\tau_1 = R_1 C_1$. For digital implementation with sample interval $\Delta t$, the discrete update is:
\begin{align}
    z_{k+1} &= z_k - \frac{\eta_c \Delta t}{Q_n} I_k, \label{eq:thevenin_ssm_z} \\
    U_{p,k+1} &= e^{-\Delta t/\tau_1} U_{p,k} + R_1\big(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau_1}\big) I_k. \label{eq:thevenin_ssm_u}
\end{align}
This model markedly improves transient accuracy over Rint while remaining efficient.

\textbf{Dual-polarization (second-order) model.} A second RC branch separates fast (charge-transfer) and slow (diffusion) dynamics:
\begin{equation}
    V_t(t) = V_{oc}\big(z(t)\big) - U_{p1}(t) - U_{p2}(t) - I(t)\, R_0,
    \label{eq:dp_terminal}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dU_{pi}(t)}{dt} = -\frac{U_{pi}(t)}{R_i C_i} + \frac{I(t)}{C_i}, \quad i \in \{1,2\}.
    \label{eq:dp_rc}
\end{equation}
Increasing model order raises identifiability risk: if $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ are not well separated, isolating individual parameters from noisy data becomes ill-conditioned.

\section{Electrochemical Physics-Based Models}
\label{sec:electrochemical}

Electrochemical models resolve transport and reaction processes with parameters that carry direct physical meaning, using porous-electrode theory with representative spherical particles for solid-phase diffusion.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Gemini_Generated_Image_y0yvhey0yvhey0yv.png}
\caption{Schematic of the Pseudo-Two-Dimensional (P2D) model, showing solid-phase particle diffusion and electrolyte transport.}
\label{fig:p2d_schematic}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Pseudo-Two-Dimensional (P2D) model.} The P2D (Doyle--Fuller--Newman) model resolves transport along an electrode-thickness coordinate $x$ and a radial particle coordinate $r$. Solid-phase diffusion follows Fick's second law,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_s(x,r,t)}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(D_s\, r^2 \frac{\partial c_s(x,r,t)}{\partial r}\right),
    \label{eq:p2d_solid_diffusion}
\end{equation}
coupled to the surface reaction rate through the Butler--Volmer relation,
\begin{equation}
    j(x,t) = a\, i_0(x,t)\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha_a F}{RT}\,\eta\right) - \exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha_c F}{RT}\,\eta\right)\right].
    \label{eq:p2d_bv}
\end{equation}
Electrolyte concentration and charge transport add further coupled equations describing ionic diffusion and conduction. Because this full DAE system is numerically expensive to solve, P2D models are mainly used offline, as high-fidelity references for validating simpler models and for detailed aging studies, rather than for real-time embedded execution.

\textbf{Single Particle Model (SPM).} The SPM simplifies P2D by dropping spatial variation in the electrolyte and representing each electrode as one spherical particle:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_{s,e}(r,t)}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(D_{s,e}\, r^2\, \frac{\partial c_{s,e}(r,t)}{\partial r}\right), \qquad e \in \{n, p\}.
    \label{eq:spm_diffusion}
\end{equation}
Terminal voltage is then reconstructed algebraically from surface concentrations and reaction overpotentials:
\begin{equation}
    V_t(t) = U_p\big(c_{s,p}^{\text{surf}}\big) - U_n\big(c_{s,n}^{\text{surf}}\big) + \eta_p - \eta_n - I(t)\, R_{e}(I,T).
    \label{eq:spm_terminal}
\end{equation}
The SPM balances physical interpretability with computational tractability, though accuracy degrades at high C-rates once electrolyte concentration gradients become significant.

\section{Thermal-Coupled Models}
\label{sec:thermal}

Internal resistance, diffusivity, and kinetic rates are strongly temperature-dependent, so electrical models are often coupled with an energy balance. The lumped thermal model is:
\begin{equation}
    m c_p \frac{dT(t)}{dt} = \dot{q}(t)\, V_{\text{cell}} - h A_s \big(T(t) - T_\infty\big),
    \label{eq:lumped_thermal}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{q}$ combines irreversible (Joule) and reversible (entropic) heat generation. Temperature-sensitive parameters such as diffusivity $D_s$ or reaction rate $k$ then feed back into the electrical model through an Arrhenius relationship:
\begin{equation}
    \Theta(T) = \Theta_{\text{ref}}\, \exp\!\left[-\frac{E_{a,\Theta}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{\text{ref}}}\right)\right].
    \label{eq:arrhenius}
\end{equation}
A spatially resolved thermal PDE is used instead of the lumped form when the Biot number indicates significant internal temperature gradients.
